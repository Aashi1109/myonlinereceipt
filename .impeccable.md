# SmartTools Design Guidance

## Design Context

### Users

- **Paperwork:** freelancers, independent contractors, solo operators, and small businesses creating invoices, receipts, expense reports, tax estimates, and related documents without needing accounting software.
- **Devtools:** developers and technical users who need quick, dependable browser utilities for inspecting, formatting, converting, and generating data.
- Users arrive with a specific task and want to finish it quickly, understand the result, and leave. They value accuracy, local processing, no login, and clear copy/export actions.
- Desktop is the primary work surface, but discovery, core actions, and results must remain usable on mobile.

### Brand Personality

- **Fast:** immediate tools, short paths, concise language, and no unnecessary setup.
- **Trustworthy:** accurate outputs, explicit validation, predictable controls, and professional presentation.
- **Private:** process data locally whenever possible and state plainly when anything leaves the device.
- The desired feeling is: “I can safely finish this task and move on.” Avoid hype, gimmicks, visual noise, and fake complexity.
- SmartTools is one brand. Platform, Paperwork, and Devtools share a foundation while adapting density and presentation to their jobs.

### Aesthetic Direction

- CodeUtilityKit is a technical and tool-scope reference only. Do not use it as a visual or brand reference for SmartTools.
- Use a restrained blue accent with neutral surfaces, strong contrast, generous whitespace, thin borders, and modest corner radii. Color should communicate hierarchy or state, not decorate empty space.
- Use a clear sans-serif for product chrome. Reserve monospace for code, structured data, identifiers, and numeric output.
- **Platform:** a light, simple discovery layer that makes projects and tools easy to scan.
- **Paperwork:** a calm light workspace with highly legible forms and trustworthy document previews. Generated document themes may vary independently from the fixed SmartTools product chrome.
- **Devtools:** a compact, workspace-first interface that follows the user’s light/dark system preference. It may be denser than Paperwork, but should remain approachable rather than imitate a terminal everywhere.
- Motion is brief and functional. Respect reduced-motion preferences; never delay a result for animation.

### Design Principles

1. **Put the task first.** Show the main input, primary action, and result before supporting content. Keep common workflows to the fewest clear steps.
2. **Make trust visible.** Explain local processing, validation, calculations, limitations, and destructive actions beside the relevant control.
3. **Use one shared visual language.** Keep typography, color roles, spacing, controls, focus states, and feedback consistent across apps; vary only what the workflow requires.
4. **Prefer clarity over maximum density.** Use readable text, persistent labels, obvious hierarchy, and plain language. Advanced options should stay available without competing with the default path.
5. **Design for real outcomes.** Copy, download, print, and export actions must be prominent, truthful, and produce output matching the preview.
6. **Handle every state.** Give empty, loading, success, warning, validation, and failure states useful copy and a clear next action.
7. **Meet WCAG 2.2 AA.** Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic controls, readable sizes, reduced motion, and status communication that does not rely on color alone.
8. **Let content support the utility.** Use categories, examples, FAQs, and guides to aid discovery and understanding without pushing the working tool out of view.

### Tool Workbench Conventions

These are product invariants, not optional layout suggestions. Preserve the
existing route and workbench when adding missing controls or states. Do not
create a parallel page, reverse established columns, or introduce a new
workbench family merely to accommodate additional functionality.

#### Shared Rules

- Extend the existing tool page in place. A new page is appropriate only for a
  genuinely new route or an explicitly requested standalone state.
- Match the implemented functionality one-to-one. Keep independent controls
  independent, do not merge settings for visual convenience, and do not invent
  unsupported options.
- Add missing settings to the established settings/options panel. Use compact
  grouping or progressive disclosure when space is limited instead of moving
  settings into the primary workspace.
- Keep the primary workspace visually dominant. Supporting guidance and related
  tools belong after the workbench, never inside the core interaction.
- Reuse the existing workbench-family components, form controls, actions,
  feedback patterns, and design tokens before creating page-specific elements.
- Show one coherent runtime state at a time. Do not display processing and
  completed output simultaneously, and do not pair an empty output region with
  a second redundant “no output” state.
- When a tool needs more than one materially different interaction stage,
  document its complete flow as horizontally ordered sibling screens:
  **initial → interaction/transition → final output**. The interaction portion
  may use multiple screens when the workspace genuinely changes.
- Each flow screen must make the triggering action, resulting UI, available
  controls, and next action obvious enough for direct developer handoff. Do not
  leave implementation-critical behavior implied between screens.
- Do not manufacture a screen for every generic empty, validation, processing,
  or success status. Reuse the shared state patterns when the primary workspace
  does not materially change, and add a sibling screen only when it does.
- Apply each route family's convention independently. Do not transfer a
  DevTools placement rule to Media or Paperwork without checking that family's
  established workflow.
- All tool routes use the canonical post-workbench supporting-sections
  component. On desktop, Limitations, Privacy, and How to use are content-sized
  cards in one horizontal row. Each card follows **category → header →
  description**, with a tighter header-to-description gap than the gap after
  the category.
- Related tools is not a card. Use the same **category → header → description**
  hierarchy on the left and vertically center the related-tool buttons on the
  right. Keep route-specific descriptions and links as component overrides.

#### End-user acceptance gate

Do not finalize a tool screen or flow because it is visually complete. Inspect
the current design as an end user and finalize it only when all of the following
can be answered **yes**:

1. **Problem fit:** Does the complete flow actually solve the job named by the
   tool, with truthful inputs, controls, previews, output, and export actions?
2. **New-user feasibility:** Can someone unfamiliar with the tool understand
   how to start, what each important setting changes, and what to do next
   without outside instructions? If not, identify the specific pain point and
   revise the flow.
3. **Five-second scanability:** Can the user quickly locate the tool purpose,
   input or uploaded file, primary action, settings, current state, result, and
   next action? The visual hierarchy must match the order of the task.
4. **Feedback and recovery:** Does every meaningful interaction communicate
   what changed, whether the input is valid, whether work is processing or
   complete, and the next valid action? Errors must explain how to recover;
   completion must expose the expected copy, download, print, or export action.

Evaluate the whole task across its horizontally ordered flow screens, not each
screen in isolation. A flow fails this gate if it contains a dead end, relies on
an unexplained icon or control, uses a generic placeholder instead of the
operation's real preview, or leaves a developer to infer a transition. Capture
and inspect the current screens before marking the flow final; fix any failed
criterion and re-check it.

Validation ownership:

- The implementing designer does not self-approve a new or materially changed
  tool flow.
- Run the repo-local `ui_validator` agent for visual continuity, hierarchy,
  density, responsive behavior, and screenshot-visible accessibility risks.
- Run the repo-local `end_user_validator` agent for problem fit, new-user
  feasibility, five-second scanability, feedback, recovery, and complete
  developer handoff.
- Both validators are read-only and must inspect current screenshots in user
  order. Resolve their substantive findings and re-run them before calling the
  flow final.

#### DevTools

- The main input, transformed output, generated values, comparison, preview, or
  other result workspace belongs on the **left**.
- All options and settings belong in the **right panel**, including newly added
  controls. Generator configuration such as length, count, character sets, and
  exclusions is still settings and therefore remains on the right.
- Result content and result-level actions such as copy-one, copy-all, and
  download-output belong with the left result workspace. The right panel may
  contain the run/generate action and validation related to its settings.
- Preserve specialized main-workspace structures—such as two comparison
  editors or input/output editors—inside the left side rather than flattening
  every tool into a generic form.

#### Media

- Uploads, file queues, page or image previews, selection canvases, and visual
  editing surfaces belong on the **left**.
- Tool settings and the process/apply action belong in the **right panel**.
- Processing feedback, completed status, and the final download action also
  belong in the **right panel**. Do not move the final Media download block into
  the left preview workspace.
- A populated Media design should replace a generic upload-only center with a
  representative visual preview whenever the operation is inherently visual.
- For Media tools that require a file and produce a downloadable result, the
  handoff flow should normally read left-to-right as:
  **initial upload → uploaded file and actual tool interaction/preview → final
  output preview with download in the right panel**.
- Keep the shared Media component limited to structural chrome. The left
  workspace must be tailored to the real operation—page thumbnails, crop
  handles, ordering, comparison, safe areas, or another truthful visual
  interaction—not an icon-only or universal placeholder canvas.

#### Paperwork

- Persistent form fields, document navigation, and editing controls belong on
  the **left**.
- The live document or report preview belongs on the **right** and remains
  visible while editing.
- Print, PDF, and export actions stay associated with the document preview.
  Avoid replacing the editor/preview workspace with a marketing-style hero or
  a long stack of disconnected cards.
